Engineering MeadowHawk

MeadowHawk has to do more than fly. Its structure, power system, flight controller, mission computer, camera, payload bay, and ground station all have to work together and still leave the safety pilot a reliable way to take over.

Overview Airframe Propulsion and power Avionics and autonomy Perception and payload Safety
Architecture

Why we chose a QuadPlane

A conventional fixed-wing aircraft needs room to launch and recover. A multirotor can take off almost anywhere, but it uses more energy in forward flight. MeadowHawk combines the two: four lift motors handle vertical flight, and a rear pusher is intended to carry the aircraft through fixed-wing cruise.

The Pixhawk 6X handles stabilization and navigation. A Jetson Orin NX runs the higher-level mission and vision software. The aircraft also carries separate pilot and ground-station links, a downward-facing camera, and a payload bay near the center of gravity.

  • Vertical launch and recovery without a runway
  • Fixed-wing cruise for longer missions
  • Waypoint navigation and search planning
  • Independent manual takeover and telemetry
  • Camera-based target work and relief-payload delivery
Subsystem 01

Airframe and aerodynamics

The airframe carries the lift system, batteries, avionics, camera, and payload. It also has to survive field handling and break down into pieces the team can transport and repair.

Wing and cruise design

We started with wing-loading, stall-speed, drag, and energy estimates, then used eCalc to compare propulsion and endurance choices.

VTOL integration

The carbon lift structure is physically H-shaped. ArduPilot uses an X-frame control allocation because that model better matches the motors' positions around the center of gravity. The July correction changed the controller setup and motor mapping, not the shape of the booms.

Design for manufacture

We split the CAD into parts that could be printed, machined, assembled, and replaced. The structure changed as the real mass, wiring, and subsystem packaging became clearer.

Subsystem 02

Propulsion and electrical power

The lift and cruise motors do different jobs. The lift system needs controllable thrust at low airspeed; the rear pusher is sized around cruise thrust, current draw, and propeller pitch speed.

  • The design uses three 6S 4500 mAh batteries in parallel.
  • A high-current distribution board feeds the four lift ESCs and the cruise ESC.
  • The July 25 proof flight used four DJI 4216 KV310 lift motors.
  • We compare calculations and eCalc results with bench tests and flight logs.

The DJI motors were an interim proof-flight set. The TDR selected T-Motor MN501-S KV360 motors and P18 x 6.1 propellers for later testing, but that combination had not yet been flight-tested.

Subsystem 03

Avionics, autonomy, and communications

The Pixhawk keeps the aircraft stable and follows the flight plan. The Jetson handles mission logic and image processing, and the two computers exchange information through MAVLink.

Flight control

ArduPlane's QuadPlane firmware provides the VTOL modes, waypoint missions, guided flight, return-to-launch behavior, and failsafes. We use Mission Planner to configure the aircraft, build missions, watch telemetry, and review logs.

Companion computer

The Jetson Orin NX runs our C++ and Python mission software. It reads vehicle telemetry, tracks mission state, and processes camera images without taking over the Pixhawk's stabilization work.

Control and telemetry

ExpressLRS carries the safety pilot's control link. A separate 915 MHz telemetry radio connects the aircraft to the ground station.

Simulation

Before trying new routines on an aircraft, we run them with ArduPilot SITL, Gazebo, and Mission Planner. That gives us a safer place to check connections, arming behavior, flight modes, and waypoint handling.

Subsystem 04

Perception, mapping, and payload delivery

The competition mission calls for finding targets, estimating their locations, and sending the correct relief object to each one. Those steps are developed and tested separately before we connect them into one mission.

Camera and detection

A downward-facing e-Con camera sends images to the Jetson. Recorded-data tests detected tent and mannequin targets. Live detection and geolocation-error testing on MeadowHawk had not yet been completed.

Modular payload bay

The payload bay sits near the center of gravity to limit trim changes. MEP flights demonstrated commanded payload release. MeadowHawk had not yet demonstrated target-relative delivery.

Safety and verification

We move up one test at a time

New work starts in calculations, simulation, or bench testing before it reaches a flight vehicle. We keep MEP results separate from MeadowHawk results, review telemetry after each flight, and recheck the configuration before moving to the next step. Manual takeover, return modes, geofencing, and preflight checks are part of that process.
